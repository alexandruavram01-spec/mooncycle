MOONCYCLE — INSIGHT CARDS FOR HER — en-GB (first batch, for preselection)
Draft — 21 October 2026
Written from the NHS and US Office on Women's Health pages listed at the end, in our
own words, each card with its source. Not reviewed by a clinician yet: a GP,
gynaecologist or midwife must check this before launch.

========================================================================================
HOW THE CARDS WILL WORK
========================================================================================
• Her Today screen shows one card a day, two at most:
  - a card for something she logged today (cramps, low mood...), if there is one,
  - otherwise a card for where she is in her cycle, rotating day by day.
  Safety cards (bleeding between periods, a late period...) come first.
• Her phone chooses the card. Nothing she logs is sent anywhere for this.
• Every card shows its source as a link ("Source: NHS").
• She can close a card; it won't come back for a while.
• "Shown when" says what makes a card appear. Fertility cards appear only on the
  fertility journey or when she turned on fertile days; "trying to conceive" cards
  only when that's her goal.
• The phone numbers (111, 999, Samaritans) are UK-only. The International edition
  will follow once you've chosen, with general wording instead.

HOW TO MARK (same as the partner list)
• [x] keep  •  [ ] drop  •  edit the wording in place  •  new cards start with [x] NEW
• Keep the codes (I01...).

Layout of each card:
  Title
  Text
  See a GP if:     ... (only on some cards)
  Get help now:    ... (only on safety cards; shown in a highlighted box)
  Source:          S-numbers, listed at the end

========================================================================================
PART 1 — HER CYCLE (one a day, by phase)
========================================================================================

---------------------------------------- PERIOD DAYS ----------------------------------

[ ] I01  Easing period pain
         Shown when: period days
         Warmth helps: a warm bath, or a hot water bottle wrapped in a tea towel on your
         tummy. Gentle movement such as walking, swimming or yoga can ease cramps too, and
         a pharmacist can tell you which painkillers suit you.
         See a GP if: the pain stops you doing your usual things, or your periods are
         getting more painful.
         Get help now: if the pain is severe or worse than usual and painkillers haven't
         helped, call 111 or ask for an urgent GP appointment.
         Source: S2

[ ] I02  Is my period heavy?
         Shown when: period days; also when she logs a heavy flow
         Signs of a heavy period: changing a pad or tampon every 1 to 2 hours, needing
         two products at once, clots bigger than a 10p coin, bleeding through to clothes
         or bedding, or a period lasting more than 7 days. Heavy periods are common, and
         there are treatments that help.
         See a GP if: heavy periods are affecting your life, or you often feel tired or
         short of breath.
         Source: S3

[ ] I03  Tampons and cups: change them on time
         Shown when: period days (every few cycles)
         Follow the time on the pack and don't leave a tampon or cup in longer than
         recommended. Wash your hands before and after.
         Get help now: if you suddenly feel very unwell while using one — a high
         temperature, feeling shivery, a rash or flu-like symptoms — take it out and
         call 111. Call 999 if you're confused, struggling to breathe, or have a rash
         that doesn't fade under a glass. This is rare (toxic shock syndrome) but serious.
         Source: S9

[ ] I04  Your period, in numbers
         Shown when: period days
         Periods usually last 2 to 7 days, about 5 on average, and are heaviest in the
         first 2 days. Most people lose 20 to 90 ml of blood, about 1 to 5 tablespoons.
         Source: S1

[ ] I05  Feeling drained?
         Shown when: period days with tiredness logged, or a heavy flow logged
         Heavy periods are one of the most common causes of low iron. Signs include
         tiredness, feeling short of breath, a racing heartbeat, paler skin and headaches.
         Iron-rich foods: dark green leafy vegetables, beans and lentils, fortified
         cereals, meat and dried fruit.
         See a GP if: you think your iron might be low. A simple blood test checks it.
         Source: S10

---------------------------------------- AFTER THE PERIOD -----------------------------

[ ] I06  A few months of logging tells a story
         Shown when: after-period days
         Note the first day of each period, how heavy it was and any pain. After a few
         months you'll see your pattern, and it's exactly what a GP will ask about if you
         ever need to see one.
         Source: S19, S1

[ ] I07  What's a normal cycle?
         Shown when: after-period days
         A cycle runs from the first day of one period to the first day of the next.
         Most are around 28 days; anything from 21 to 35 days is common.
         See a GP if: your cycles are often shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, or
         your periods last more than 7 days.
         Source: S1, S5

[ ] I08  Cervical screening
         Shown when: after-period days (about twice a year)
         If you're 25 to 64 and have a cervix, the NHS invites you for cervical screening
         every 5 years. When your letter arrives, book it.
         Source: S18

---------------------------------------- MID-CYCLE (fertility cards) ------------------

[ ] I09  Around ovulation
         Shown when: mid-cycle, fertility journey or fertile days on
         Ovulation usually happens 10 to 16 days before your next period. Around then,
         discharge often turns clear, thin and stretchy, like raw egg white.
         Source: S7

[ ] I10  Twinges on one side?
         Shown when: mid-cycle; also when she logs "Ovulation pain"
         Ovulation pain is common: a dull ache or sharp twinges on one side of the lower
         tummy, lasting from a few minutes to a day or two. A warm bath or a painkiller
         from the pharmacy is usually enough.
         See a GP if: it keeps coming back or painkillers don't help.
         Get help now: if the pain is severe and painkillers haven't eased it, or you
         think you could be pregnant, call 111 or ask for an urgent GP appointment.
         Source: S8

[ ] I11  Fertile days are an estimate
         Shown when: mid-cycle, fertile days on, goal is not "trying to conceive"
         Sperm can survive up to 7 days, and cycles can shift, so pregnancy is possible
         on days before the predicted ovulation. A calendar estimate is not contraception:
         a GP or contraception clinic can help you choose a reliable method.
         Source: S7, S1

---------------------------------------- BEFORE THE PERIOD ----------------------------

[ ] I12  The days before a period
         Shown when: before-period days
         Many people notice changes in the days before a period: mood swings, tiredness,
         bloating, tender breasts, headaches, spots or cravings. They can vary from month
         to month.
         Source: S4

[ ] I13  What can help with premenstrual symptoms
         Shown when: before-period days
         Regular exercise, small meals every few hours, enough sleep, and something that
         helps you unwind, such as yoga or meditation. Less alcohol and not smoking can
         help too.
         Source: S4

[ ] I14  Your log is your evidence
         Shown when: before-period days
         Logging how you feel for at least 2 cycles shows whether symptoms follow your
         cycle, and it's what a GP will want to see.
         See a GP if: symptoms affect your daily life, or don't improve with changes like
         exercise and sleep. There are treatments.
         Source: S4

========================================================================================
PART 2 — WHAT SHE LOGGED TODAY
========================================================================================

[ ] I15  Cramps today
         Shown when: she logs "Cramps"
         Warmth on your tummy or back, a warm bath or a gentle walk can ease cramps.
         See a GP if: cramps stop you doing your usual things, or you also have pain
         during sex or when going to the toilet. These can be signs of conditions such
         as endometriosis, which can be treated.
         Source: S2, S15

[ ] I16  Headache
         Shown when: she logs "Headache"
         Headaches are a common premenstrual symptom. Keep logging them: if they follow
         your cycle, that's useful for you and a GP to know.
         Source: S4

[ ] I17  Bloated?
         Shown when: she logs "Bloating"
         Bloating is common in the days before a period. Small meals every 2 to 3 hours
         can help some people.
         Source: S4

[ ] I18  Tender breasts
         Shown when: she logs "Breast tenderness"
         Breast pain linked to periods is common and usually eases when the period ends.
         A well-fitting bra, and a soft one at night, can help, and so can a painkiller
         from the pharmacy.
         Get help now: a hard lump that doesn't move, nipple discharge, a change in breast
         shape, skin like orange peel, or redness and heat need an urgent GP appointment
         or a call to 111.
         Source: S20

[ ] I19  Tired today
         Shown when: she logs "Fatigue" outside period days
         Tiredness is a common premenstrual symptom, and getting plenty of sleep helps.
         If you're often tired and your periods are heavy, ask a GP to check your iron.
         Source: S4, S10

[ ] I20  Go gently today
         Shown when: she logs "Sad", "Anxious", "Irritable" or "Sensitive"
         Mood changes before a period are common. Movement, sleep and time to unwind can
         help. If it's intense every cycle, it may be PMDD, a more severe form of PMS that
         a GP can help with.
         See a GP if: low mood or anxiety is affecting your daily life.
         Get help now: if you feel you can't keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E.
         Samaritans are there to talk to any time on 116 123.
         Source: S4, S17

[ ] I21  Bleeding between periods
         Shown when: she logs spotting or bleeding outside her period days
         Bleeding between periods or after sex should always be checked by a GP or a
         sexual health clinic. There are many possible causes, contraception among them.
         Get help now: if you've recently missed a period and have unusual bleeding with
         tummy pain, ask for an urgent GP appointment or call 111.
         Source: S14

PART 3 — HER CYCLE OVER TIME
========================================================================================

[ ] I22  Period late?
         Shown when: her period is 3 or more days late, goal is not "trying to conceive"
         Periods can come early or late. Stress, weight changes, a lot of exercise and
         some contraception can all shift them. If you could be pregnant, most tests work
         from the first day of a missed period.
         See a GP if: you've missed 3 periods in a row, or a test is negative twice and
         your period still hasn't come.
         Source: S6, S13

[ ] I23  Waiting to test?
         Shown when: her period is late, goal is "trying to conceive"
         Most pregnancy tests work from the first day of a missed period. A negative
         result is less reliable than a positive one: if your period still hasn't come,
         test again in a few days.
         Source: S13

[ ] I24  A longer period than usual
         Shown when: she has logged bleeding for more than 7 days in a row
         Periods usually last 2 to 7 days. If yours often last longer, it's worth seeing
         a GP.
         Source: S1, S5

[ ] I25  Your cycles vary
         Shown when: 2 of her last 3 cycles were shorter than 21 or longer than 35 days
         Cycles under 21 days or over 35 count as irregular. Stress, weight changes,
         exercise, contraception or conditions such as PMOS (formerly PCOS) can cause it.
         See a GP if: your periods are irregular, especially with weight changes,
         tiredness, extra facial hair or skin changes, or if you're trying to get pregnant.
         Source: S5

========================================================================================
PART 4 — TRYING TO CONCEIVE (only when that's her goal)
========================================================================================

[ ] I26  Folic acid
         Shown when: goal is "trying to conceive" (early in each cycle)
         Take 400 micrograms of folic acid every day while you're trying, and until
         you're 12 weeks pregnant. Some people need a higher dose on prescription, for
         example with diabetes or a family history of spina bifida: ask a GP.
         Source: S16

[ ] I27  Timing without pressure
         Shown when: goal is "trying to conceive"
         The NHS's simple advice: have sex every 2 to 3 days without contraception. More
         than 8 in 10 couples where the woman is under 40 conceive within a year this way.
         Source: S11, S12

[ ] I28  When to see a GP
         Shown when: goal is "trying to conceive", trying for 6 months or more
         The NHS suggests seeing a GP after a year of trying, or sooner if you're 36 or
         over, or already know of a possible fertility problem.
         Source: S12

[ ] I29  Looking after you both
         Shown when: goal is "trying to conceive"
         Cutting out alcohol and stopping smoking help when you're trying. A GP can
         support you with either.
         Source: S11

[ ] I30  Egg-white days
         Shown when: goal is "trying to conceive", she logs "Egg white" discharge
         Clear, stretchy discharge like raw egg white usually means you're around
         ovulation, when pregnancy is most likely.
         Source: S7

========================================================================================
PART 5 — ANY DAY
========================================================================================

[ ] I31  When periods need a check
         Shown when: any day (every few weeks)
         Changes in your periods are worth a check. Bleeding between periods, after sex,
         or after the menopause always needs a doctor.
         Source: S1
